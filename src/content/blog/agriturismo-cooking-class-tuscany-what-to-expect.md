Search for a cooking class in Tuscany and you get two different things wearing the same name. One is a class in a city cooking school, three hours, walk in off the street. The other is a day on a working farm in the hills, where the first thing you do is walk out to the garden.

We cook in both. Endri and Marsel are the head chefs of two agriturismi in the hills outside Florence and also teach in our own city kitchen in the Oltrarno, so this is a comparison we make most weeks. Here is what the farm version actually involves, and who it does not suit.

## What an agriturismo actually is

An *agriturismo* is a working farm licensed to host guests. The licence is the important part: Italian law ties it to real agricultural activity, so an agriturismo is a farm first and a hospitality business second. That is why the olive oil on the table is usually the estate's own, and why the menu changes when the garden does.

It also means the range is enormous. Some agriturismi are a few rooms above a working barn. Others are restored estates with a pool, a restaurant and a wedding licence. Both are legitimately agriturismi, and the cooking class on offer at each will feel nothing like the other.

## How a farm class differs from a city class

| | Class in Florence | Class at an agriturismo |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Two to three hours | Usually half a day |
| Getting there | On foot | Car or an arranged transfer, 30 to 45 minutes from Florence for the nearest hills |
| Where the food comes from | The morning market | Partly the estate's own garden |
| Group size | Small, often capped under ten | Larger, because farm kitchens and tables are |
| The meal | Lunch or dinner at one table | A long lunch, usually outside if the weather allows |
| Priced | Per person | Often per group |

The craft is the same in both. Flour, eggs, your hands, a board. What changes is the half-day shape of it and the fact that on a farm the menu is decided partly by what is ready rather than entirely by the chef.

## What the day usually looks like

Most farm classes follow the same arc, and it is worth knowing it so you can plan the rest of the day around it honestly rather than optimistically.

You arrive mid-morning. Someone walks you round the part of the garden that is producing, and what is ready decides some of what you will cook. Then you are at the bench for the bulk of it: dough, resting, rolling, shaping, a sauce built properly rather than reheated. Somewhere in there a loaf or a focaccia goes in, because the oven is on anyway.

Then you sit down, and that is where a farm class stops behaving like a lesson. The table is long, the wine is from somewhere you can see, and nobody is waiting for the room. Allow more time than the listing says. The eating is not the epilogue.

## Six things to check before you book

1. **Who is teaching.** On a farm this matters more than in a city school, because "the chef" can mean the estate's working cook or a freelancer hired for the afternoon. Ask whose kitchen it is.
2. **Whether the farm is actually farming.** Some estates kept the licence and dropped the agriculture. If the garden is the selling point, ask what is in it this month.
3. **The exact address, and when you get it.** Several operators, us included, work with more than one estate and confirm which one with your date. That is normal, but you should know the driving time before you commit to a day.
4. **Who is driving.** The lunch includes wine, the roads are unpaved and unlit, and this catches people out more than anything else on this list.
5. **Whether a room is included.** It usually is not, even when the estate has rooms. Two bookings, two businesses.
6. **What happens if it rains.** Half of the appeal is outdoors. Ask where lunch moves to.

## Who an agriturismo class is not for

If you have one day in Florence, do not spend it on a car journey. A half-day in the hills plus transport is most of your daylight, and the pasta you learn is the pasta you would have learned in the city. If you are two people rather than a group, per-group pricing works against you. And if nobody in the party can drive, the transfer cost often changes the maths entirely.

For those cases the city class is simply the better buy, which is why we teach both and say so. ## Who it is for

Groups. Families travelling together, friends who rented a villa, a company that wants a day rather than an activity. The farm format earns its extra half-day when there are enough of you that a city table cannot hold the party, and when the point of the day is the day rather than the lesson.
