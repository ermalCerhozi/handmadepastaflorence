When people try to make fresh pasta at home outside of Italy, they often grab whatever flour is in the pantry, crack an egg into it, and hope for the best. Sometimes it works out, but often the dough is tough, dense, or tears easily. 

The secret to that silky, perfect bite you get in a Tuscan *trattoria* isn't a complex recipe. It's using the right flour. 

In Italy, there are two main flours used for pasta, and they each serve a very specific purpose. 

## 1. Type "00" Flour (Farina 00) - For Fresh Egg Pasta

If you are making fresh egg pasta shapes like [pappardelle](/pasta-shapes/pappardelle/), [tagliatelle](/pasta-shapes/tagliatelle/), [ravioli](/pasta-shapes/ravioli/), or [tortelli](/pasta-shapes/tortelli/) you need **Type 00 flour**.

"00" (pronounced *doppio zero*) does not refer to the protein content of the flour, but rather to how finely it is milled. The Italian grading system goes from 2 (coarse and whole wheat) down to 00 (the finest, whitest flour). 

Because 00 flour is milled so finely, it feels almost like talcum powder. When combined with the fat and protein from fresh eggs, it creates a dough that is incredibly silky, elastic, and smooth. This elasticity is crucial when you are trying to roll the dough out into a sheet so thin you can read a newspaper through it.

**Can you use All-Purpose flour?** 
Yes, but your pasta won't be as delicate. All-purpose flour has a slightly coarser grind and usually a different protein profile, leading to a dough that is slightly chewier and harder to roll out completely thin.

## 2. Semolina Flour (Semola di Grano Duro) - For Water Pasta

If you are making a southern Italian shape like *orecchiette* or a rustic Tuscan shape like [pici](/pasta-shapes/pici/), you leave the eggs in the fridge. For these, you need **Semolina flour** and warm water.

Semolina is milled from hard durum wheat (*grano duro*). It has a pale yellow color and feels much coarser, almost like fine sand. Because durum wheat is very hard and high in gluten-forming proteins, it creates a very strong, structured dough even without the binding power of eggs. 

Pasta made with semolina has a fantastic, hearty chew to it. It also has a rougher surface that grips onto thick, rustic sauces brilliantly. 

*Note: You will also often see pasta makers dusting their wooden boards with semolina flour when rolling out 00 egg dough. Because semolina is coarse and dry, it acts like tiny ball bearings, preventing the fresh egg pasta from sticking without being absorbed into the dough.*

## The Perfect Blend

In some regions (and even in our own kitchen depending on the humidity and the shape we are making), pasta makers will blend the two. A common mix is 70-80% 00 flour and 20-30% fine semolina (*semola rimacinata*). This gives you the silky workability of the 00 flour, with just a bit of the structural bite and yellow hue of the semolina.
