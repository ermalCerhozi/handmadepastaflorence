Most day-trip lists from Florence are organised by what you can photograph. **This one is organised by what is on the plate**, because the towns around Florence do not cook the same food Florence does, and the differences are the actual reason to go.

**Easiest by train:** Lucca and Pisa, direct from Santa Maria Novella.
**Best for a food-led day:** Siena and Lucca.
**Needs a bus change:** San Gimignano, via Poggibonsi.

## Siena: pici, and the pasta Florence does not make

Siena is the one town that fills a whole day on its own, and it has the single most distinctive pasta in Tuscany.

**[Pici](/pasta-shapes/pici/)** are thick strands rolled between the palms, made from flour and water with **no egg** — peasant pasta, in the honest sense: made when eggs were worth more sold than eaten. They are uneven by definition, and that is the point; the sauce catches on the irregularities. Order them ***all'aglione***, with a long-cooked garlic and tomato sauce, or ***con le briciole***, with toasted breadcrumbs standing in for cheese.

The sweets are the other reason. **Ricciarelli** are soft almond biscuits, chewy rather than crisp. **Panforte** is dense, spiced, fruited and about a thousand years old. Both travel well, which matters if you are buying presents.

If you are drinking, you are in reach of **Brunello di Montalcino** and **Vino Nobile di Montepulciano**, both about an hour further south.

## Lucca: tordelli, and a town you can walk around the top of

Lucca is flat, walled, and far less crowded than Siena. You can walk the full circuit of the Renaissance walls in about an hour, which is the rare Tuscan attraction that is free and requires no queue.

The food reason is **tordelli lucchesi**, and the naming trips up almost everyone. Lucca's tordelli are **meat-filled half-moons served with a meat ragù**. They are not the same as the [tortelli](/pasta-shapes/tortelli/) you meet in the Mugello and Casentino, which are filled with **potato**. Same corner of the same region, two different dishes, near-identical names.

Also worth ordering: **zuppa di farro**, the spelt and bean soup that is the local everyday dish, and **buccellato**, a sweet aniseed and raisin bread that Lucchesi give as a gift and slice for breakfast.

## San Gimignano: white wine, saffron, and the earliest crowds

The towers are the photograph; the drink is the reason to stay for lunch. **Vernaccia di San Gimignano** was the first Italian wine to receive a DOC, and it is a proper dry white in a region that is overwhelmingly red. Order it where it is grown.

The other local product is **saffron**, cultivated here since the Middle Ages and still grown on a small scale. You will find it in risotto and in pasta sauces on local menus.

The honest warning: San Gimignano is small and receives a very large number of coaches. Arrive before ten or after four, and it is a different town.

## Pisa: cecina, and two hours that are enough

Pisa is the shortest of these and the easiest to underestimate. The Campo dei Miracoli takes about two hours including the tower, and then most visitors leave without eating anything specific to the city.

What they miss is **cecina** — also called *torta di ceci* — a thin, baked chickpea flatbread, sold hot by the slice, salty and faintly bitter, often eaten folded inside a bread roll. It costs almost nothing and it is the single most Pisan thing on offer.

Pisa and Lucca sit on the same railway line, about half an hour apart, which makes them the obvious pair for one day.

> One practical note that catches people out: regional trains are cheap and frequent but unreserved, and if you buy a **paper** ticket you must validate it before boarding. E-tickets bought in the app do not need validating. The fine for an unvalidated paper ticket is not small and inspectors on the Lucca and Pisa lines are diligent.

## Getting there without a car

- **Pisa Centrale** — about 60 minutes, direct and frequent from Firenze Santa Maria Novella.
- **Lucca** — about 80 minutes, direct.
- **Siena** — the coach is the better answer. Siena's railway station sits well below the historic centre, so the train leaves you with a climb or a local bus; the fast coach drops you far closer to the Campo.
- **San Gimignano** — no railway. Train or bus to **Poggibonsi**, then a local bus up. Budget a little over 90 minutes each way.
- **Chianti** — genuinely needs a car, and a designated driver.

## Which one should you pick?

1. **One day, no car, want a full day:** Siena. Pici for lunch, ricciarelli to take home.
2. **One day, no car, want calm:** Lucca. Walk the walls, eat tordelli, buy buccellato.
3. **Half a day, flying out of Pisa anyway:** Pisa. Two hours at the tower, cecina by the slice.
4. **Wine first:** San Gimignano, early or late, with Vernaccia at lunch.
5. **You have a car and a free afternoon:** the countryside and a kitchen in it.

Pick one town, not three. The point of a Tuscan day trip is the long lunch in the middle of it.
