Florence is a museum city, and museums are where a day with children goes wrong. **The good news is that it is also a food city, and that is the part children are naturally good at.** Here is a day built around eating and walking rather than queueing, from people who cook here and who watch families arrive exhausted at two in the afternoon.

**Best with kids:** Boboli Gardens, the bell tower, the Mercato Centrale, gelato, the carousel.
**Hardest with kids:** the Uffizi and the Accademia, and dinner before 20:00.
**The one phrase to learn:** *pasta in bianco*.

## What to do in Florence with children

**Mornings are for height and air.** Climb **Giotto's bell tower** rather than the dome: it is 414 steps but it opens out onto landings on the way up, so there are natural places to stop and look. The **dome** is 463 steps in a single committed spiral with no lift and no turning back, which is a different proposition with a seven-year-old.

**The Boboli Gardens** are the release valve. They are big, sloped, full of gravel paths, fountains and hiding places, and children can simply move. The **Bardini Garden** next door is smaller, quieter and has the better view.

**The Mercato Centrale** works twice: downstairs is a real food market with whole fish and hanging meat, which small children find far more interesting than a painting, and upstairs is a food hall where everyone orders something different and nobody has to agree.

**Two small rituals** cost nothing and are remembered: rubbing the nose of **Il Porcellino**, the bronze boar at the Mercato Nuovo, and the carousel in **Piazza della Repubblica**.

**Piazzale Michelangelo** at sunset gives you the view, wide steps to sit on, and no requirement to be quiet.

> The honest caveat: the Uffizi and the Accademia are extraordinary and they are also an hour of queueing followed by crowded rooms where the rule is do not touch anything. If you want to do one, book a timed slot, go first thing, and do only that one. Both are closed on Mondays, along with Palazzo Pitti.

## Where to eat in Florence with children

Italian restaurants are genuinely welcoming to children; the friction is timing and portions, not attitude.

- **Schiacciata.** The flat, oil-rich Tuscan bread, split and filled, eaten walking. It is the single most useful food in this city with a hungry child, and it costs a few euros.
- **Pizza al taglio.** By the slice, by weight, no waiting.
- **The food hall at the Mercato Centrale.** Everyone picks their own. This solves the family where one child eats only plain pasta and another wants to try everything.
- **Gelato**, once a day, with the colour test: dull pistachio and grey banana mean real fruit and nuts. Fluorescent means paste.
- **A proper trattoria at lunch** rather than dinner. Lunch is cheaper, quieter, earlier, and fresh pasta is at its best because kitchens that roll their own roll it that morning.

Two pieces of restaurant vocabulary do most of the work. ***Pasta in bianco*** is plain pasta with butter or oil and parmesan on the side — what Italian parents order for their own children, so nobody will think anything of it. ***Mezza porzione*** is a half portion, normal and usually cheaper. Ask for both without apology.

And if your child is the adventurous kind, **lampredotto** from a cart is a few euros and an excellent story to take home.

## A day that actually works

1. **09:00** Bell tower, before the heat and the queues.
2. **10:30** Mercato Centrale downstairs, then a schiacciata.
3. **12:00** Cross the river. Boboli or Bardini, and let everyone run.
4. **13:30** Lunch as the main meal: pasta, half portions, no rush.
5. **15:30** The quiet hour. Back to the room, or a long gelato in the shade.
6. **17:30** Il Porcellino and the carousel, then the walk up to Piazzale Michelangelo.
7. **19:30** Something small. Pizza al taglio, or the food hall again.

Note what is not in it: two museums, and dinner at nine.

## Cooking together, which children are unexpectedly good at

The thing that surprises most parents is that a hands-on kitchen holds a child's attention far longer than a gallery does, because there is something to do with their hands every few minutes.

Our [family cooking class in Florence](/family-cooking-class-florence/) takes children **from six**, always with an adult at the table, and never more than **eight people including the children** — which is the whole reason a chef can be beside a child when a fold goes wrong. It runs about three hours, structured around the dough's own rhythm: knead, let it rest, eat something, come back. Children are €50, adults €95, and under-sixes sit and eat free rather than cook, because the boards and rolling pins are the wrong size for smaller hands. Knife work is limited and supervised, and the boiling water stays with the chefs.

## Practical notes nobody tells you

- **Heat.** From June to September the middle of the day is genuinely hard. Plan the indoor or shaded hour between 14:00 and 16:00 deliberately.
- **Water.** Public fountains across the city run cold and drinkable. Carry one bottle and refill it.
- **Pushchairs.** The cobbles are rough and the pavements narrow. A carrier is easier for anyone light enough to carry.
- **Bathrooms.** Plan them around cafés and museums; free public ones are scarce. Buying a coffee buys the bathroom.
- **Mondays.** The Uffizi, the Accademia and Palazzo Pitti are shut, which sounds like a problem and is actually an opportunity — we wrote a whole guide to [what to do in Florence on a Monday](/blog/what-to-do-in-florence-on-a-monday/).
- **The Oltrarno** is the side of the river with workshops, less traffic and more room to breathe: [things to do in the Oltrarno](/blog/things-to-do-in-oltrarno-florence/).

Plan the food, keep the museums to one, and let the afternoon be boring on purpose.
