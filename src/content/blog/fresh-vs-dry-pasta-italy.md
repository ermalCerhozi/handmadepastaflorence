If you walk into a kitchen in Italy, you won't hear anyone arguing about whether fresh pasta is "better" than dry pasta. To an Italian, that’s like asking if a spoon is better than a fork. They do entirely different jobs.

Here is the real difference between fresh pasta (*pasta fresca*) and dry pasta (*pasta secca*), and how to know which one you should be eating.

## Fresh Pasta (Pasta Fresca)

When you take a [pasta making class in Florence](/pasta-making-class-florence/), you are making fresh pasta. 

In Tuscany and the rest of Northern and Central Italy, fresh pasta is usually made with just two ingredients: **soft "00" flour and fresh eggs**. 
The dough is kneaded until smooth, rested, and then rolled out by hand with a wooden pin. It is cut into shapes like [pappardelle](/pasta-shapes/pappardelle/), [tagliatelle](/pasta-shapes/tagliatelle/), or folded into stuffed shapes like [ravioli](/pasta-shapes/ravioli/) and [tortelli](/pasta-shapes/tortelli/).

### Why use fresh pasta?
Because it contains eggs and soft flour, fresh pasta is tender, porous, and velvety. It cooks in just two or three minutes. Its soft, absorbent texture makes it the perfect vehicle for rich, heavy sauces. If you have a slow-cooked wild boar ragù, a rich butter-and-sage sauce, or a heavy cream sauce, you want fresh egg pasta to soak it all up.

*Note: In Southern Italy (and parts of Tuscany, like with our famous [pici](/pasta-shapes/pici/)), fresh pasta is often made without eggs, using just semolina flour and water, but it is still cooked right after being shaped.*

## Dry Pasta (Pasta Secca)

Dry pasta is what you buy in a box at the grocery store, but artisanal dry pasta is a serious craft in Italy. 

By Italian law, commercial dry pasta must be made from **durum wheat semolina (semola di grano duro) and water**. The dough is extruded through bronze dies (which gives it a rough surface) and then dried very slowly at low temperatures. 

### Why use dry pasta?
Dry pasta is firm. It maintains a distinct, resilient chew—the famous *al dente* bite. Because it doesn't contain egg, it won't absorb sauces in the same way fresh pasta does. Instead, its rough, starchy surface allows lighter sauces to cling to the outside. 

If you are eating spaghetti with clams (alle vongole), carbonara, a simple garlic and oil (aglio e olio), or a fresh tomato sauce, you *must* use dry pasta. Fresh pasta would turn to mush when tossed vigorously with olive oil and seafood.

## The Rule of Thumb

*   **Rich, meaty, buttery, or creamy?** Use **fresh pasta**. (Think Pappardelle al Cinghiale).
*   **Olive oil-based, seafood, or fresh tomato?** Use **dry pasta**. (Think Spaghetti alle Vongole).
