Search for a pasta class in Florence and you will get several hundred listings that read almost identically: hands-on, small group, local chef, eat what you make, wine included. The photographs are interchangeable. The prices range from about €50 to about €150 for what is described in the same words.

We teach these classes, so we have an obvious interest here; say so up front. But we also spend a lot of time with guests who booked something else first and were disappointed, and the disappointments are remarkably consistent. Here is what actually separates the classes, in the order that matters.

## 1. What is the real maximum group size?

This single number predicts your experience better than anything else on the page, and it is the one most often left vague.

Be aware of what "small group" means locally. In Florence, listings routinely use it for a class of **twelve**, and reviews of larger classes return to the same complaint: too many people, not enough attention from the chef. Twelve people around one instructor is a demonstration you are standing near, not a lesson.

Under eight is a different thing entirely. It is the point at which a chef can physically get to you in the thirty seconds when your dough is going wrong, which is when teaching actually happens.

**Ask for the number, not the adjective.** If a listing will not state a maximum, assume it is high.

*Ours is eight, and six on the market tour.*

If you are booking for a work group rather than yourself, the maximum stops being the useful number and the format does: a shared class caps out, while a [private team building class](/team-building-cooking-class-florence/) takes the whole kitchen for 6 to 14 people and runs to your agenda instead of a fixed timetable.

**Honest caveat:** if what you want is a big, loud, sociable evening where you meet a dozen travellers and the cooking is the excuse, a larger class is genuinely more fun and usually cheaper. Book one of those. Small groups are for people who want to come away actually able to make the pasta.

## 2. Is the wine in the price, or added at the end?

One of the most common gaps between the price you compare and the price you pay. Plenty of classes quote a headline figure and then offer a wine pairing at €15–€20 per person when you sit down. Two of you, and that is €40 you did not budget.

It is not dishonest; it is just easy to miss when you are comparing two listings side by side at midnight.

**Ask: is wine included, how much, and is there anything else added on the day?**

*Ours includes two Tuscan pours in the €95, and there is nothing added at the end.*

## 3. Who is actually teaching you?

"Professional chef" and "local host" are not the same job, and the listings use them interchangeably.

There is nothing wrong with a well-trained host running a well-designed class. But if you are paying at the upper end, you are entitled to know who is in the room and what they do when they are not teaching tourists. A named chef with a traceable working history is a different proposition from an unnamed "our chefs".

**Ask: who teaches this class, and what do they cook when they are not teaching?**

*Ours are [Endri Cerhozi](https://endricerhozi.com) and Marsel, head chefs of two agriturismi in the hills outside Florence. Pasta for weddings and feasts is the day job; the class is where we teach it.*

## 4. How is gluten-free actually handled?

This is where the range between classes is widest, and where a vague answer should worry you.

A real answer sounds like: a dedicated gluten-free flour blend, a separate board and tools, its own pot of cooking water, and a station set up before the room fills with airborne wheat flour. A non-answer sounds like "yes, we can accommodate that."

Three things worth knowing before you book anywhere:

- **Some classes charge extra for it.** Ask. It is flour, not truffle.
- **Some decline severe allergies outright**, because the kitchen and the tools are shared and cross-contamination is genuinely possible. That refusal is responsible, not unhelpful; take it seriously rather than shopping until someone says yes.
- **Almost no cooking school is a certified gluten-free facility.** A pasta kitchen has wheat flour in the air. Anyone promising you a zero-risk environment in a room where other people are rolling semolina is overselling.

*Ours: dedicated blend, own board, own tools, own water, set up before class, no surcharge, and you sit at the same table as everyone else. We are also straight about the limit: it is a working pasta kitchen and we are not a certified facility. [The full explanation is here](/gluten-free-cooking-class-florence/).*

## 5. Do you eat what you make, and do you take anything home?

Most Florence classes end with a meal, but check whether it is *your* pasta or a plated dish that appeared from the back. The difference matters more than it sounds: eating the thing you made badly is the point of the exercise.

Then ask the smaller question almost nobody thinks to ask: **do you leave with anything?** A class you enjoyed and cannot reproduce is an afternoon, not a skill. Recipes sent afterwards are the minimum.

*Ours: you eat what you made, we email the recipes afterwards, and whatever you do not finish gets dried, bagged and goes back to your hotel with you.*

## 6. Can the children genuinely take part?

There is a real difference between a class that *permits* children and a class where a child gets their own board, their own dough and a chef who checks on them.

Both exist in Florence and both describe themselves as family-friendly. The tell is group size again: at a table of twelve, a seven-year-old is a spectator no matter how welcoming the listing sounds.

**Ask: what age, what does my child actually do, and what do they pay?**

*Ours takes children from six, each with their own station, at €50 for ages 6–12 and free under six. [Details here](/family-cooking-class-florence/).*

## 7. What does the price actually include?

Work out the real total before comparing, because the headline number is rarely the whole thing. Check for:

- Wine, or a pairing added at the table
- A gluten-free or dietary surcharge
- An apron or equipment fee
- Whether the meal is a full sit-down or a tasting
- Whether recipes and any take-home are included
- VAT: normally included in Italy, but confirm it

Then compare like with like. A €95 class including wine, a full meal and no surcharges is cheaper in practice than a €110 class that adds €18 for wine and charges for the gluten-free option.

## The short version

If you only ask two questions, ask **what is the maximum group size** and **what is not included in the price.** Those two answers separate the good classes from the disappointing ones more reliably than anything else on the listing page.

And whichever you book, get the cancellation terms in writing before you pay.
