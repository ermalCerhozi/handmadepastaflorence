Short answer, with the numbers: **tagliatelle is cut 6–8 mm wide, pappardelle 20–30 mm.** Same egg dough, same rolling, same pot. The only difference is where you put the knife, and that difference decides which sauce works.

I cut both most days, so let me be more useful than "one is wider".

## Every Italian ribbon, by width

| Ribbon | Width | Home | Built for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tagliolini** | ~2 mm | Piedmont, Liguria | Broth, butter, seafood |
| **Fettuccine** | ~6 mm | Rome | Butter and cheese, thicker than it looks |
| **Tagliatelle** | 6–8 mm | Bologna, Tuscany | Meat ragù, porcini |
| **Pappardelle** | 20–30 mm | Tuscany | Wild boar, hare, duck |
| **Lasagne** | Sheet, uncut | Everywhere | Layered, baked |

That is the whole family. They are all *pasta a nastro*, ribbon pasta, and they are all the same dough. If someone hands you a plate and you are not sure what you are looking at, measure it with your eye against your finger: a finger is about 15 mm, so pappardelle is noticeably wider than one and tagliatelle is half of one.

## How wide is pappardelle, really?

Two to three centimetres. Two fingers.

Tuscans are not precious about the exact number, and you will find kitchens cutting anywhere from 20 to 30 mm, but the shape stops being pappardelle somewhere around 15 mm. Below that you have made wide tagliatelle, which is a fine thing to have made, just not this.

The name comes from *pappare*, to gobble. It is a Tuscan verb and it is not polite. Nobody named this pasta after the ribbon; they named it after what happens when the boar ragù arrives.

## How wide is tagliatelle, really?

6 to 8 mm, and Bologna will give you the exact figure whether you asked for it or not.

There is a brass rule deposited at the Bologna chamber of commerce that fixes the ideal cooked tagliatella at 8 mm. Cooked is the operative word: egg dough takes on water and swells, so you cut at roughly 6 to 7 mm raw to land at 8 mm on the plate.

You do not need the brass rule. You need to cut consistently, because ribbons of different widths cook at different speeds and you end up with some al dente and some soft in the same forkful.

## Why the width is not a decoration

A sauce has to have something to hold on to, and the amount of surface a ribbon offers changes what will stay on it.

A wild boar ragù is chunky. The pieces of meat need a flat surface broad enough to sit on, or they part company with the pasta and settle at the bottom of the bowl, which is how you end up eating plain pasta first and a puddle of sauce last. That is what the 30 mm is for.

A finer ragù, or porcini in butter, does not need that much landing area, and on a ribbon that wide it looks lost. Tagliatelle gives it the right ratio.

This is the entire logic of Italian pasta shapes, and it is worth internalising because it makes every future decision obvious: **the chunkier the sauce, the more surface it needs.** Our [shapes library](/pasta-shapes/) works through the same reasoning for all four shapes we teach.

## Cutting them by hand

Roll the sheet, flour it, then fold it loosely into a flat roll about 8 cm across. Do not press the fold; you want it to unroll cleanly.

Then cut across the roll with a sharp knife in one confident motion. Hesitate and you drag the dough and get a ragged edge. For tagliatelle, cut at the width of a pencil. For pappardelle, cut at the width of two fingers.

Unroll immediately, before the ribbons have time to think about sticking to each other, and drop them into loose nests dusted with semolina. Fresh egg ribbons cook in two to three minutes, sometimes less, so have the sauce ready before the pasta goes in and not the other way round.

The one mistake to avoid: rolling the sheet too thin. Ribbons want a little body, especially pappardelle, which has to hold a heavy sauce without tearing. If you can read a newspaper through it, you have gone too far. That thinness belongs to [filled shapes like tortelli](/blog/ravioli-vs-tortelli/), and even there it is a mistake.

## So which one do you want?

Boar, hare, duck, anything with big pieces in it: **pappardelle**. Fine meat ragù, mushrooms, butter, anything smooth: **tagliatelle**.
