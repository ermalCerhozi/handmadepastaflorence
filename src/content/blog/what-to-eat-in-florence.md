You came to Florence partly for the food, and then you opened a menu with forty things on it and no way to tell which of them the city actually eats. **Plenty of what is served to visitors in the centre is Italian, good, and from somewhere else entirely.** Here is what is genuinely Florentine, from someone who cooks it for a living.

**What to eat:** bistecca alla fiorentina, lampredotto, ribollita, pappardelle al cinghiale, crostini di fegatini.
**What is Tuscan pasta:** pici, pappardelle, tagliatelle, potato tortelli.
**Where it is cheapest and best:** standing at a counter at lunch, not sitting down at dinner.

## What are the classic Florentine dishes?

Six plates carry the city, and you can eat all of them in three days.

**Bistecca alla fiorentina** is the famous one: a T-bone three fingers thick, salted, grilled over embers, served rare and sold by weight. It is priced per kilo for a reason. It is a dish for two or three people, it is not meant to be well done, and a restaurant that offers to cook it through is telling you something about itself.

**Lampredotto** is the one locals actually eat at lunch. Tripe, the fourth stomach, simmered in broth and stuffed into a bread roll with salsa verde, from a cart. It costs a few euros. It is milder than it sounds.

**Ribollita** and **pappa al pomodoro** are the winter and the everyday: both are bread soups, thickened with yesterday's unsalted Tuscan loaf, one with black cabbage and beans, the other with tomato and basil. **Panzanella** is the same principle in summer, served cold as a salad.

**Crostini di fegatini** are the thing that arrives before everything else: chicken liver pâté on toast, dark and slightly sweet. Ordering them marks you as someone who has eaten here before.

**Peposo** is the slow one: beef shin cooked for hours with black pepper and Chianti, a dish supposedly fed to the kiln workers who fired the tiles of Brunelleschi's dome.

> One habit worth knowing: Tuscan bread has no salt in it. This is not a mistake and it is not you being unlucky. It exists to be eaten with salty things — salumi, pecorino, liver pâté — and to be the backbone of the soups above.

## What pasta is actually Tuscan?

This is the part visitors get wrong most often, because the pasta that is famous internationally is mostly from Emilia or Rome.

- **[Pici](/pasta-shapes/pici/)** are the most Tuscan shape there is: thick strands rolled by hand, no egg, no machine, from around Siena. Usually served with garlic and breadcrumbs (*all'aglione*, *con le briciole*) or a duck ragù.
- **[Pappardelle](/pasta-shapes/pappardelle/)** are wide ribbons that exist because Tuscany hunts. They are built to hold **cinghiale** (wild boar), hare, or a long-cooked beef ragù. Wide pasta, strong sauce.
- **[Tagliatelle](/pasta-shapes/tagliatelle/)** are the narrower sibling, better with lighter sauces and mushrooms.
- **[Tortelli](/pasta-shapes/tortelli/)** in the Mugello and Casentino are filled with **potato**, not ricotta. Butter and sage, or a meat ragù over the top. Most visitors have never seen them before and remember them afterwards.

If a menu in the centre offers all four plus carbonara plus lasagne plus penne arrabbiata, it is a menu written for tourists rather than a kitchen with a point of view. That is not automatically bad. It just is not Florence cooking for itself.

## Which famous Italian dishes are not from Florence?

None of these are fake and none of them are bad. They are simply from other cities, and knowing which is which makes a menu readable:

- **Spaghetti bolognese** is not served in Bologna, where ragù goes with tagliatelle. Bologna is in Emilia-Romagna, not Tuscany.
- **Fettuccine Alfredo** is a Roman dish that became an American one.
- **Carbonara**, **cacio e pepe** and **amatriciana** are Roman.
- **Lasagne** as most people know them are Emilian.
- **Tiramisù** comes from the north-east, near Treviso.

You will find all of them in Florence, cooked well. Just do not travel here for them.

## What to eat at each meal

**Breakfast** is not a meal here in the way you may be used to. A cappuccino and a pastry standing at the bar, before eleven, and that is it.

**Lunch** is where Florence is cheapest and most itself: lampredotto or a **schiacciata** (the flat, oil-rich Tuscan bread, split and filled) from a counter, eaten standing. If you sit down, lunch is also when fresh pasta is at its best, because most kitchens that roll their own roll it that morning.

**Aperitivo** runs roughly from six to eight. A negroni — invented in this city — or a glass of Chianti, with something salty alongside.

**Dinner** starts late by northern European standards, rarely before eight. This is when the bistecca makes sense, and when a proper four-course meal is worth the time.

**Afterwards:** **cantucci** dipped in **vin santo**, or gelato from a shop where the pistachio is dull green rather than bright, and the banana is grey. Bright colours mean paste, not fruit.

## What to eat in Florence: a three-day plan

1. **Day one, lunch:** lampredotto from a cart near the Mercato Centrale. **Dinner:** crostini di fegatini, then pappardelle al cinghiale.
2. **Day two, lunch:** a schiacciata standing up, then gelato. **Dinner:** bistecca alla fiorentina, shared, with a Chianti Classico and nothing else complicated.
3. **Day three, lunch:** ribollita or panzanella depending on the season, and potato tortelli if you see them. **Evening:** aperitivo in the [Oltrarno](/blog/things-to-do-in-oltrarno-florence/), then cantucci and vin santo.

Eat the bread with something salty, order the pasta at lunch, and let the bistecca stay rare.
