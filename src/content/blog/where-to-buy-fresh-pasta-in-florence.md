People ask me this more than almost anything else, usually on their last day: where can I buy pasta like the one I just ate, and can I take it home? Here is the honest answer, from someone who rolls it for a living.

## Where can you buy fresh pasta in Florence?

Three places, in order of how much I trust them:

1. **A pastificio.** A shop that makes fresh pasta on the premises and sells it by weight. This is where Florentines buy the tortelli for Sunday lunch.
2. **The market stalls.** Both the [Sant'Ambrogio market and the ground floor of the Mercato Centrale](/blog/florence-food-markets-sant-ambrogio-mercato-centrale/) have fresh-pasta sellers. They trade in the morning, Monday to Saturday.
3. **A good gastronomia or alimentari.** Many delis keep a tray of fresh pasta from a local maker beside the cheese counter. Ask who made it: a good shop will tell you.

What I would skip: the sealed trays of *pasta fresca* in the supermarket fridge. They are industrial, pasteurised for a long shelf life, and perfectly fine on a weeknight. They are not what you came to Florence for.

## How do you spot a real pastificio?

- **You can see the work.** A table, a sheeter or a rolling pin behind the counter, flour on somebody's forearms. If the pasta arrives in a delivery van, it is a shop, not a pastificio.
- **The shapes change.** Pappardelle in autumn when the boar ragù comes back, ravioli with whatever greens are good this week. A counter that looks identical every day of the year was filled from a factory.
- **Filled pasta is sold by weight and made that day.** Look at the edges of the ravioli: hand-sealed ones are slightly irregular.
- **There is a queue on Saturday morning.** Sunday lunch is the fresh-pasta meal of the week here, so Saturday morning is when a good pastificio is busiest. Most of the city's food shops close on Sunday, which is worth knowing if you are planning [a Sunday in Florence](/blog/what-to-do-in-florence-on-a-sunday/).

If you searched "fresh pasta near me" from a hotel in the centre, look for the words *pastificio* or *pasta fresca* on the sign, and go in the morning: by late afternoon the best shapes are gone.

## How much should you buy?

Everything is sold by the *etto*, 100 grams. For ribbons such as [tagliatelle](/pasta-shapes/tagliatelle/) or [pappardelle](/pasta-shapes/pappardelle/), allow about one etto per person as a first course, one and a half as a main. For [filled pasta](/pasta-shapes/ravioli/), allow one and a half to two etti. "Tre etti, per favore" is a perfectly good sentence.

## Pasta to go: eating it on the move

Florence has caught up with the idea of pasta in a box. A number of small counters in the centre now cook fresh pasta to order and hand it over to take away, and the food hall upstairs at the Mercato Centrale, open every day, has hot pasta among its stalls.

It is a good lunch on a bench between two museums. Two tips from the kitchen side: short and filled shapes survive a box better than long ribbons, which keep cooking in their own heat and go soft, and a sauce with some oil or butter in it holds up better than a thin tomato one. For a proper sit-down plate, my [guide to handmade pasta in Florence](/blog/where-to-eat-handmade-pasta-in-florence/) is the better read.

## How long does fresh pasta keep?

- **Egg ribbons:** one to two days in the fridge, loosely dusted with flour in a paper bag, not sealed plastic, or they sweat and stick.
- **Filled pasta:** the same day or the next. The filling is wet, and the dough softens around it.
- **Freezing:** both freeze well. Spread them on a floured tray, freeze, then bag them. Cook straight from frozen; never thaw them first.

## Can you take fresh pasta home on a plane?

Within Europe, on a short flight, with a cool bag: yes, and it will be fine for dinner that evening. Beyond that, two problems. Fresh pasta is perishable, so a long-haul day in a warm bag is not kind to it. And filled pasta is usually stuffed with ricotta, cheese or meat, and many countries restrict meat and dairy in personal luggage. Check your own country's rules before you pack a kilo of ravioli.

The better souvenir is **good dried pasta**: artisanal, bronze-cut (*trafilata al bronzo*) and dried slowly, which gives it the rough surface that holds sauce. It travels in a suitcase, keeps for a year and is what most Italian kitchens use on a weekday anyway; I explain the difference in [fresh vs dry pasta](/blog/fresh-vs-dry-pasta-italy/). Add a bag of Italian flour (here is [which flour to buy](/blog/best-flour-for-italian-fresh-pasta/)) and you can make the fresh kind yourself.
